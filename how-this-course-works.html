How This Course Works

This page is the map: what this course is, how class time works, and where everything lives.

About this website

This site is the public companion of the Canvas course (WFA 4863/6863, Mississippi State University, Fall 2026). It exists to make the course's resources easy to reach — weekly pages, readings lists, lab guides, and materials, open in any browser without logging in. Everything official happens in Canvas: assignment submissions, quizzes, discussions, grades, announcements, and the full syllabus (open the course and choose Syllabus in the course menu).

Course materials follow WCAG 2.1 AA accessibility standards; if anything is hard to access, tell me and I will fix it.

What this course is

Principles of One Health is a hands-on course. It introduces systems thinking and the connections among the pillars of One Health — human health, domestic-animal and wildlife health, and environmental health — and teaches you to trace the mechanisms that link them. You won't memorize lists; you'll learn to see systems, map them, and reason about where to intervene.

How classes run

This course breaks a little with the traditional lecture format and it depends in large part on you:

  • Companion readings come first. Each week's readings are short, chosen, and expected before you walk in. They're the raw material of the conversation.
  • Class time is for discussion-based learning. Sessions are built around engaging with the ideas together — questions, arguments, tracing pathways out loud. Active engagement isn't a bonus; it's the course.
  • You don't need to transcribe. Lectures are recorded and their transcripts are processed into study guides delivered to you — so your attention goes to participating, not note-taking. (Your Obsidian vault is for connecting ideas during your personal and group work sessions, not for capturing lecture words verbatim.)
  • The course is about the learning journey not content memorization. One Health is an approach to solve problems. It requires a way of thinking, not memorization of content. I know it sounds odd, but you will see that if you focus on learning, the grades will not matter.

The rhythm of every week

  • Monday (12:00–12:50) — new concepts for the week are introduced.
  • Wednesday (12:00–12:50) — we go deeper into processes, and their components connecting them.

  • Wednesday lab (2:00–3:50) — in groups, you apply the week's concepts: developing systems maps of real study cases.

  • Systems Check (Canvas discussion) — each week, individually, you trace one mechanistic pathway from the week's topics and post it as a diagram with a written description. It's your weekly rep of exactly the thinking this course examines — mechanisms, feedbacks, and context.

Labs: hands-on and digital at once

Lab work runs on two tracks at the same time: analog — collaborative, hands-on systems-mapping techniques, working around a shared canvas — and digital — documenting that thinking in a scalable, durable way with the course toolkit.

Every lab produces a deliverable, and the semester's deliverables are assessed with a specifications-grading approach: clear specs, met or revised, building your competencies week over week rather than averaging points.

The early arc: Lab 1 sets up your software toolkit; Lab 2 covers literature searches and search engines; from Week 3 we start reviewing literature and case studies and drawing systems via causal loop diagrams (CLDs).

The three arcs of the semester

  1. Foundations — Weeks 1–5. Systems thinking, ecosystem services, social determinants of health, climate, food & water. Exam 1 in Week 5.
  2. Zoonotic Interfaces — Weeks 6–11. Spillover, wildlife reservoirs, outbreaks, surveillance, risk, and AMR. Exam 2 in Week 11.
  3. Making It Real — Weeks 12–15. Governance, collaboration, risk communication, policy — ending with a public presentation of your group work.

Assessment at a glance

Assessment differs by section: undergraduates (4863) complete two exams, a collaborative research review, and a group presentation; graduate students (6863) complete exams, a data synthesis and visualization, a research review and synthesis, a policy brief, and an oral defense. Lab deliverables follow the specifications-grading approach. Official dates, point values, and policies live in the Canvas and will be discussed and agreed in collaboration between the instructor and students.

Distance students

Distance students join lectures live on Webex and complete labs through the same interactive tools and shared workspaces as on-campus teams — the toolkit was chosen so that collaboration works the same from anywhere.
