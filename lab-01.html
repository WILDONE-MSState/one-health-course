Today's Goals

  1. Understand why each tool in the course toolkit earns its place
  2. Install and configure all four tools: Obsidian, Excalidraw, Google Drive, and Paperpile
  3. Test live collaboration with your team in Excalidraw and Google Drive
  4. Annotate your first paper in Paperpile

Why This Lab Matters

Today we build your workbench. One Health work is collaborative by nature — teams spread across disciplines, institutions, and time zones — and these four tools are how this course teaches that way of working. Work through this guide with your team and help each other past the hiccups: install problems compound, and Lab 2 assumes today's toolkit works. Nothing here is busywork; each "why" below tells you how the tool earns its place.

Step 1: Obsidian — notes that don't go static

Why. Most notes die the moment you write them: collected once, never revisited, never connected. What we want instead is a knowledge map that grows over time — you capture content and your own thoughts, and you connect them across pieces of documentation. This matters enormously in One Health: you might read a news story about an outbreak in the morning, and weeks later connect it to a peer-reviewed article — and that connection, written down and linked, is the beginning of an analysis. Your vault becomes a system of your own knowledge, built the same way you'll learn to map any other system.

Do (10 minutes)

  1. Download Obsidian at https://obsidian.md and install it (Windows, macOS, Linux; there's a mobile app too).
  2. On first launch, choose Create new vault, name it one-health, and put it somewhere you'll find again (a vault is just a folder of plain markdown text files — yours forever, no lock-in).
  3. Create your first note titled One Health and write two sentences on what you think it means today. You'll enjoy re-reading this in Week 15.
  4. Make your first link: type [[ inside any note and start typing another note's name. Then open the graph view (button in the left sidebar) — that's your map. It has two nodes today; watch what it looks like by December.

Step 2: Excalidraw — thinking in systems, together

Why. A systems diagram is how we make connections visible: boxes for the components, arrows for the relationships between them, signs on the arrows for the direction of effect. Drawing one forces you to say exactly what is connected to what, and how — which is the core skill of this course. Excalidraw is our standard tool for it because it's free, it runs in the browser, and its live collaboration rooms let your whole team draw on one shared canvas at once — in the room or across town.

Do (5 minutes)

  1. Open https://excalidraw.com — it runs immediately, no account needed. (Signing in enables saved files and collaboration rooms; do it.)
  2. Draw three boxes, connect them with arrows, and double-click an arrow to label it. That's 80% of what you'll need.
  3. Test collaboration: one teammate clicks the share/live collaboration button and sends the link; everyone joins and adds a box to the same canvas.

Step 3: Google Drive — one file, many hands, no chaos

Why. One Health work is collaboration across teams, institutions, and time zones — people who can't sit in the same room need to work on the same files. Do that by emailing attachments and within a week you have final_v2_REAL-final.docx and nobody knows which version is true. Google Drive solves this: one shared file, everyone works in it, every change tracked, conflicts resolved by the platform instead of by email archaeology.

Do (5 minutes)

  1. Sign in to Drive with your university Google account.
  2. When teams are assigned, one member creates the folder WFA-4863-<TeamName> and shares it with all teammates and the teaching team with edit access.
  3. Inside, create four folders: labs/, research-review/, presentation/, scratch/.

Learn the modes (2 minutes — this is the part people skip and regret). Google Docs and Slides have three modes, top-right of the window:

Mode What happens When to use it
Editing Changes land directly Your own drafting
Suggesting Changes appear as tracked suggestions to accept/reject Improving a teammate's section
Viewing Read-only Reviewing without touching

Add comments (select text → + icon) to discuss rather than overwrite, and if something goes wrong, File → Version history shows every prior state of the document — nothing is ever truly lost.

File naming: YYYY-MM-DD_short-description_vN — e.g., 2026-09-16_causal-loop-food-security_v2. Future-you will be grateful.

Step 4: Paperpile — your library of evidence

Why. This course runs on peer-reviewed literature, and you need a system for collecting, reading, annotating, and citing it — that's a reference manager. Paperpile lives in your browser, stores each paper with its full metadata and PDF, formats your citations automatically, and lets you highlight and annotate PDFs so your reading leaves a trace you can use.

First, one concept: the DOI. A DOI (Digital Object Identifier) is a permanent fingerprint attached to a published paper — it looks like 10.3390/tropicalmed4020088. Paste a DOI into doi.org, a search engine, or Paperpile, and it resolves to that exact paper, forever, no matter where the journal's website moves it. When in doubt about which paper someone means, ask for the DOI.

Do (10 minutes)

  1. Go to https://paperpile.com and sign up with your university Google account (free 30-day trial; academic licensing is inexpensive — talk to me if cost is a barrier).
  2. Install the Paperpile browser extension (Chrome-based browsers: Chrome, Edge, Brave).
  3. Connect it to Google Drive when prompted — Paperpile stores every PDF in a Paperpile folder in your Drive, so your library and your files live together and sync everywhere.
  4. Create a folder WFA-4863-One-Health, with subfolders week-01 … week-15 and research-review.

Your first annotation (we'll do this together). Find this week's reading — Mackenzie & Jeggo (2019), DOI 10.3390/tropicalmed4020088 — via the DOI. Click the Paperpile button to save it (watch the metadata and PDF arrive on their own), file it into week-01, open the PDF in Paperpile's viewer, highlight one sentence that surprises you and attach a note saying why. That highlight-plus-note is the unit of annotated reading you'll repeat all semester.
