Your Toolkit

The four tools on this page are not an afterthought — they are assembled deliberately as your One Health workbench. Real One Health work is collaborative by nature: teams spread across disciplines, institutions, time zones, and continents. These tools are how this course teaches that way of working — collaboration, discussion, and active group engagement — while building skills you will keep using long after the semester ends. You'll install all four in Lab 1 — the step-by-step walkthrough lives in the Lab 1 guide; this page is the standing reference for what each tool is for and how we use it.

Obsidian — your course notebook

Obsidian is a free note-taking app whose core feature is linking notes to each other — a systems-thinking tool disguised as a notebook. In this course it's also how you learn to work in markdown and plain-text files: notes that no company can lock away, that version well, and that assemble into a knowledge map that grows over time. By December, your vault will literally be a map of One Health.

How we use it: one note per lecture topic and per reading (title = author + year); link related ideas by typing [[ — every [[link]] is you asserting a relationship you should be able to explain. Before each weekly deliverable, open the graph view and look at what your notes connect to — orphan notes are unfinished thinking.

Paperpile — managing the literature

Paperpile is a reference manager that lives in your browser and syncs with Google Drive. You'll use it to collect, read, and cite the peer-reviewed literature of this course — and to learn evidence-based synthesis: it integrates with Google Docs so your team's documents cite properly with no hand-typed references.

How we use it: when you open a journal article, one click saves it with its PDF and full metadata; file each reading into its week folder; for team writing, a shared folder + the Google Docs plugin handles in-text citations and the bibliography.

Excalidraw — drawing systems

Excalidraw is a free, browser-based whiteboard — our standard tool for causal loop diagrams, stock-and-flow sketches, and systems maps, from the first mapping labs through your final presentations.

Conventions: arrows carry signs — + (same direction) or − (opposite); mark reinforcing loops R and balancing loops B. Export as PNG and always include a written description of the diagram — an accessibility requirement for everything we share, and the best test of whether your diagram says what you think it says.

Google Drive — the team workspace

Google Drive (through your university account) is where team work lives: lab deliverables, the research review, presentation drafts, and Paperpile's PDFs. Learning to run a shared workspace well — naming, versioning, commenting, working asynchronously — is exactly the skill you'll need to collaborate on (or lead) One Health projects that span teams, time zones, and countries.

Conventions: file names YYYY-MM-DD_short-description_vN (e.g., 2026-09-16_causal-loop-food-security_v2); work in shared Docs/Slides rather than emailing attachments — comments and version history are part of how teams coordinate.

One rule across all four tools

Everything shared or submitted is accessible: real heading styles in documents, descriptive link text (never "click here"), alt text on images, and a written description with every diagram.
